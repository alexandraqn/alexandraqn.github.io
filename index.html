Job Market Paper · Single-Authored
The Impact of Expanding Child Health Insurance on Parental Labour Supply: Evidence from Mexico’s Seguro Popular Universal Health Insurance
Single-authored Working Paper
Universal health insurance reforms in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are designed to mitigate catastrophic health expenditures, yet they also alter household economic constraints, precautionary labor supply, and domestic caregiving burdens. This paper evaluates the labor supply impacts of expanding public health insurance coverage to children under five in Mexico through Seguro Popular. Using a sharp Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) at the five-year age eligibility cutoff across 447,355 individual-quarter observations from the Mexican National Employment Survey (ENOE 2007–2008), I estimate the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) of eligibility under flexible bandwidth specifications. While I find no aggregate effect on overall parental labor force participation, significant gendered heterogeneities emerge. Mothers of children near the eligibility cutoff increase their labor supply intensity by an average of 2 hours per month (p < 0.10). These findings indicate that expanding child health coverage generates intra-household spillovers by alleviating maternal caregiving burdens and unlocking secondary labor supply.

Working Papers & Research in Progress

The Effect of Public Health Insurance on Labour Supply: Evidence from Indonesia's Askeskin Programme
with Dr. Julia Hatamyar
Public health insurance reshapes household economic incentives, creating a tension between financial risk protection and improved labor productivity. This study evaluates the labor market impacts of Indonesia’s subsidized health insurance program, Askeskin. Utilizing panel microdata from the Indonesia Family Life Survey (IFLS 2000 and 2007) comprising 13,497 individuals (26,994 person-year observations), we deploy a doubly robust two-period difference-in-differences estimator combining propensity score reweighting with outcome regression to control for self-selection into enrollment. Exposure to Askeskin reduces weekly labor supply at the intensive margin by 1.3 hours (p < 0.10), with no adverse impact on aggregate labor force participation or wage income. Disaggregating these effects reveals strong heterogeneities: weekly working hours decline by 2.3 hours among women (p < 0.01) and by 2.7 hours among household heads (p < 0.10), consistent with a reallocation of time toward home production and care-seeking once financial health risks are covered. Crucially, among baseline informal workers, Askeskin coverage increases the probability of transitioning to formal employment by 2.5 percentage points (p < 0.01) and reduces informal employment retention by 2.9 percentage points (p < 0.10). These findings demonstrate that public health insurance reduces precautionary labor supply while acting as a subsidy for labor market formalization in developing economies.

Hospitalisations and Within-Household Labour Reallocation: Evidence of Family Spillovers in Indonesia
with Dr. Julia Hatamyar and Prof. Luigi Siciliani
While health insurance expansion has increased hospital utilization across low- and middle-income countries, sudden hospitalization shocks continue to threaten household economic stability through labor supply disruptions and caregiving burdens. Using cross-sectional microdata from the 2019 Indonesian National Socio-Economic Survey (SUSENAS), this paper examines how personal and familial hospitalization events affect individual employment participation and hours worked. We estimate linear probability and OLS models with city fixed effects, controlling for demographic and household covariates, and verify identification using Augmented Inverse Probability Weighting (AIPW) and Coarsened Exact Matching (CEM). We uncover stark gender asymmetries in intra-household labor adjustments. Living with a hospitalized family member reduces a woman's employment probability by 2.0 percentage points, rising to 2.8 percentage points when two or more family members are hospitalized. Furthermore, women residing with multiple hospitalized relatives reduce their labor supply by 1.29 hours per week. Conditional on employment, a woman's own hospitalization is associated with an increase of 0.6 working hours per week. In contrast, while a man's own hospitalization reduces his employment probability by 1.9 percentage points, male labor supply remains entirely unaffected by family hospitalizations. These results provide empirical evidence that family health shocks induce substantial labor supply spillovers, with caregiving responsibilities transferred disproportionately onto women even within expanding insurance regimes.
